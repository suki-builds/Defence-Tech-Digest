# Fox and Lion Writing Style

Scope: analysis and news writing only. Outreach, Korean-language copy, events, social platform conventions and formal documents are excluded.

Labels used below:
- **Stated**: given by Suki directly.
- **Inferred**: interpretation of examples or chat summaries, not stated by Suki.
- **Unconfirmed**: needs a decision.

---

## 1. Rules for both analysis and news

**Stated**
- Serious, accessible, non-tribal, formal British and understated.
- British English spelling and punctuation.
- No em dashes.
- Spaced en dashes are acceptable but must be used VERY sparingly.
- No contractions.
- No "It is not A, it is B" constructions.
- Write "U.S." never "US".
- Never describe Fox and Lion as a "think tank". Use "Analysis", "network" or similar.
- Brevity over filler. Avoid phrases that state the obvious.

---

## 2. Analysis

**Stated**
- Pace is slower and measured, not punchy.
- Spaced en dashes are more acceptable here than in news, where an argument is being built up. They remain sparing.

**Inferred** (from two example paragraphs, on the Royal Navy and HMS Dragon, and on the peace dividend)
- Long, multi-clause sentences that build an argument step by step.
- A paragraph opens with a concrete case or a broad historical frame, then draws the wider point from it.
- Evaluative words are used sparingly but with weight, for example "stark" and "gutted".
- Normative claims are stated plainly.
- Figures and time spans anchor the claims, for example "over the last 50 years" and "from over 7% of GDP to barely above 2%".
- An occasional short closing sentence for emphasis.
- Country names written in full in running prose ("the United States", "the United Kingdom"), based on two paragraphs only.

**Reference paragraphs (Suki's own text, unaltered)**

> A recent saga provides a stark illustration of how thin the navy is stretching itself – the case of HMS Dragon. In early March, HMS Dragon was deployed to Cyprus to defend British bases from direct drone strikes. However, this decision – to spare a single ship – was an agonisingly slow, weeks-long, highly publicised political affair, with the Prime Minister directly ordering the movement of a single ship. The deployment of a single ship should not, and has never been, a prime minister-level decision. Yet here we are now.

> Over the last 50 years, the United States has risen to become the dominant military power around the world. This has allowed western Europe to reap a "peace dividend" under the presumed protective blanket of NATO. Successive British governments have gutted the British Armed Forces with the assumption that should European security ever find itself in peril, the United States will step in to protect its NATO brothers. As a result, while the United Kingdom has spent roughly the same amount on defence since the 50s in real terms, while defence spending has tumbled from over 7% of GDP to barely above 2%.

Note: both paragraphs may contain small slips (for example the double "while" in the second, and a possibly missing word after "has never been" in the first). They are retained here unaltered as reference text.

## 3. News

**Stated**
- Less conversational and more relay-like than analysis.
- Spaced en dashes should be rarer still than in analysis.
- Non-English article titles always receive an English translation, in British English.
- Summaries of opinion or analysis pieces must make clear that they reflect the author's views, not reported fact.
- Sponsored or advertorial pieces are skipped. General "Sponsored by" or "Underwritten by" show banners do not make an article sponsored.

**Metadata and summary format (stated)**
- SEO title: under 60 characters, checked.
- SEO description: two sentences, under 150 characters.
- Summary: up to three bullets, maximum 25 words each, no labels, plain language.

**Inferred**
- Plain statement of who did what, when and how much, with no editorial colour. This follows from "relay-like" but was not spelled out.

**Article body (drafted by Claude, agreed by Suki on 28 October 2026)**
- Every news item has a body after the summary: 150 to 300 words, in short paragraphs of one to three sentences. Shorter when the sources are thin; never pad.
- The first paragraph gives the most important fact: who did what, when and how much. Context and background follow, then what happens next.
- The order and emphasis are our own, led by what matters to UK, European and Korean readers. Never follow the structure of a source article.
- Facts come only from the sources, combined where there are several. No speculation, no editorial colour and no closing comment or summing-up line.
- No direct quotes. What officials or companies said is paraphrased and credited to the speaker, for example "the Ministry of Defence said".
- Source outlets and their reporters are never named or credited. The copy does not refer to reporting or missing detail ("reportedly", "according to reports", "no further detail was given").
- Organisations are named in full on first mention, with a short form after if needed ("the Ministry of Defence", then "the MoD").
- The body may repeat facts from the summary, but not its sentences.
- Exclusive reporting is not rewritten: if an outlet says it has learned something, obtained documents or spoken to unnamed sources, the item is skipped.

## 5. Pre-publication check

1. Contractions: none.
2. Em dashes: none. Spaced en dashes: only where essential.
3. "It is not A, it is B": none.
4. "U.S." not "US".
5. British spelling throughout.
6. Foreign-language titles translated into English.
7. News: title under 60 characters, description under 150 characters, bullets at 25 words or fewer.
8. News: body of 150 to 300 words, no direct quotes, nothing copied from a source.
